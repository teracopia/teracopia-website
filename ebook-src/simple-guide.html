What is Lucid Dreaming?

We all sleep; we all dream. Most of us don't remember our dreams; instead, we wake up and get on with the day without a second thought for what occurred during the hours we were asleep. Everyone dreams, even if we don't recall the experiences of each dream, often reaching up to six dreams within a single night.

A lucid dream is a dream state in which you become conscious of the fact that you're dreaming. Once you become lucid, your dreamworld is now your playground, where you have full autonomy to fly like a superhero, walk through walls, and communicate with your higher self without the restrictions imposed by the physical world.

Lucid dreaming is one of the most joyful and rewarding experiences you can give yourself, taking the hours you spend sleeping unconsciously and transforming them into spectacular journeys of exploration. If you need more proof, some athletes have reported using lucid dreaming to mentally rehearse specific techniques as part of their training, practicing their craft while they dream to sharpen their preparation.

Indeed, lucid dreaming is a known and well-researched phenomenon; however, most people do not actually practice lucid dreaming and instead choose to forfeit their untapped sleeping real estate to be randomized, or more likely, completely forgotten by the time they awaken. We are going to flip that model and equip you with the tools to sit in the driver's seat of your dreams whenever you choose.

This isn't anecdotal either, multiple researchers from Stanford University demonstrated in sleep lab studies that participants could lucid dream and signal to observers in real time through pre-arranged eye movements while their brain activity was being recorded, proving real evidence that lucid dreaming is a distinct and verifiable state of consciousness, not simply a feeling or trick of the mind.

Your Subconscious Mind

Dreams are a random, sporadic culmination of experiences, feelings, and lessons that come to us coincidentally and without much explanation. Many have tried to decode and reconstruct their dreams as a compass for their lives, and while dreams certainly can shed light on specific areas of life, the nuances of dream interpretation and meaning are beyond the scope of this guide, and instead should be evaluated on an individual and personal level.

What we do know about dreams is that they come from the subconscious mind, which is a layer of your mind beneath your objective, waking mind we coexist with on a daily basis. Our subconscious mind receives impressions from our conscious mind, without having the reason to determine if the contents are true or valid, and instead takes orders directly from the conscious mind and in turn influences our dream state.

This is why, oftentimes, dreams are extremely random and originate from a moment in our waking life which we simply glanced at and did not give our full attention to. In fact, it's quite normal to have a dream focused on something small and unimportant that occurred during our waking hours; meanwhile, our most pressing circumstances and moments in waking life are often nowhere to be found in our dreams.

The point here is to remember that the subconscious mind is quite impressionable, and to be extra thoughtful in how we pass down information, judgements, and feelings from our conscious mind onto our subconscious mind. Your waking mind may know something to be a joke or not taken seriously; however, your subconscious mind does not have the same privilege.

Aim, therefore, to be a guardian and gatekeeper of what you think daily, and do not permit negative or hurtful thoughts to linger in your mind. Focus instead on the positive counterpart, acknowledging negative thoughts and choosing to maintain a calm and positive state of mind.

Lucid Dreaming Purposes

There's really no shortage of reasons why someone might want to lucid dream, but let's look at a few main pillars for what you could potentially benefit from this practice.

Recurring Dreams & Nightmares

Lots of people experience nightmares and recurring dreams on a regular basis, and usually it's under negative circumstances. Whether it's you reliving a particularly painful or traumatic point in life, or a deep-seated fear which was never resolved, your subconscious mind is the ultimate scorekeeper, and will remember whatever is imprinted upon it by your conscious mind.

Lucid dreaming is incredibly powerful because we have an opportunity to activate our conscious mind in an environment constructed by our subconscious mind. That is to say, anything you experience in a dream is a creation of your subconscious, and you have the ability to look those fears or negative feelings right in the eye and have a meaningful conversation towards resolving it.

If you find that you've been subject to nightmares or recurring dreams that put you into a state of fear or unease, chances are it's better to work on solving them at the root rather than turning a blind eye. If you're experiencing blockages in life that exist below the surface and you can't seem to crack the code on consciously working through them, lucid dreaming is a great place to start addressing those issues and finding an actual solution.

In an environment where every person, place, and thing is fabricated by your own unconscious mind, you can become lucid in your dreams to roleplay, act out scenarios, even directly asking your subconscious mind for help in navigating these situations, where you can access your higher self, free from judgement or blame. Indeed, lucid dreaming is one of the great equalizers for shedding light on the darkest parts of our being, and you have the opportunity to explore them in a safe and private realm whenever you'd like.

Rehearsing Skills and Performance

Lucid dreaming is useful for much more than working through fear or an uncomfortable recurring dream, it's also a training ground for whatever skill or performance you want to practice in your waking life. Because your subconscious mind can construct a fully immersive environment with physics, sensations, and consequences, you can use your dreams to rehearse any technique or practice you want, in the same way an elite athlete visualizes their routine before stepping onto the field. Some high- performing athletes even swear by lucid dreaming specifically to hone their craft, with coaches and trainers leaning into this practice to optimize an athlete's recovery and preparation at the same time.

Learning the ability to control your environment and rehearse your craft over and over isn't limited to sports either, whether you're a musician working on a difficult piece, a speaker polishing a public speech, or even someone working through a creative block on their next big project, lucid dreaming gives you a private and personal arena to take your craft and run different scenarios, try a different approach, or see how you feel before you even attempt it in your waking life. The mind doesn't always separate a rehearsed dream from a vivid memory from the past, so use this to your advantage by setting an intention to work on a specific skill or scenario the next time you're dreaming, and use your dreams as an extension of your waking practice.

Self-Exploration and Growth

Beyond some of the more practical uses, lucid dreaming is truly one of the most direct paths towards understanding your own subconscious mind, and by extension, your own self. Once you're able to consciously bend your dreams under the direction of your willpower, you gain access to a newfound space that is entirely constructed by your inner being, free from social pressure and judgement from your waking life. Lucid dreaming is an incredibly powerful tool for self-reflection and internal harmony, you can ask questions about your life, observe reactions to different scenarios, and notice the finest details in what your subconscious is trying to tell you.

Lucid dreaming is the first step towards aligning with your higher self, and for many, it's opening the door to exploring consciousness even further beyond the dream state.

BUILDING YOUR PRACTICE

The Dream Journal

The first step towards lucid dreaming is to start by keeping a dream journal. Simply keep a notebook by your bedside and make an effort to start every morning by immediately writing down whatever you can recall from your dreams the night before. Since we average multiple dreams per night, chances are we also tend to forget most, if not all of these dreams because we make zero attempt to recall our experiences and record them.

Using Technology

One suggestion for your dream journal is to physically write down your dreams instead of using a phone or computer, the reason being when you write with your hand on pen and paper, you are subconsciously rewarding your brain for giving you the thought in the first place. In this way, we reward our mind for remembering our dreams, so that we can begin to reprogram our mind to consistently remember our dreams.

Having dreams written on your phone or computer does not serve you, plus there is the benefit of putting pen to paper and actually forming each word with intention, as opposed to a keystroke, which does not carry the same feeling or intent.

If you prefer to keep an audio recording of your dreams, you may do so, however it's not suggested to do so because you'll likely be accessing a recording device on your phone, which will accelerate the rate at which you forget your dreams. One other note is to make sure you are prioritizing your dream journal as soon as you wake up, it should be the first conscious action you take to start off your day.

Delaying the process of writing down your dreams, even just to check your phone for a few moments, will have a negative impact on your natural dream recall ability, and also sets a negative feedback loop in your subconscious mind by deprioritizing your dreams, giving your mind little incentive to actively recall them.

Treat a dream journal like you would an alarm clock. Make it a priority to write down dreams as they come, and soon you'll build a foundation to remember more details of your dreams, full experiences instead of short scenes, and eventually you will get into the habit of waking up excited to document and record your dreams, observing patterns, recurring themes, and areas to work on in the future.

At the start, you may only remember fragments of a dream: feelings, characters, settings, objects, all are good enough to be written down. Start with bits and pieces, work up to bullet points, and eventually full sentences.

You don't need full sentences for the log to be successful either, and it doesn't even need to make sense. A perfectly good early entry might read something like this:

“Was back in my old apartment, the hallway kept going. A dog I don't recognize followed me. Felt anxious but not scared. Someone was talking but I couldn't hear the words. Woke up before anything else happened.”

If you find that you're waking up each morning and unable to remember your dreams, you must lie still in your bed until those fragments of memories start to come together. If, however, you get up out of bed and start your day, the chances of you remembering even a sliver of a dream drop significantly.

Remembering Dreams

You might also find that you remember the end of a dream, but not the beginning. This is totally normal, and when you're recording a dream after you've woken up, try this method. If you can only remember how a dream ended right before you woke up, try to logically piece together what happened just before that point. You will likely uncover a small detail of what occurred right before the dream ended, or what prompted you to get to that point. String together the moments until you can remember the scene, then keep going backwards as far as you can.

There is a possibility that interactions throughout the day may spark a memory from your dream the night before; we do not want to rely on these random occurrences, and if anything, you should treat them as a bonus to add to your dream journal.

Don't have your dream journal after you've left the house or started your day? Write down your dream on a piece of paper and make sure to add it into your dream journal when you have time. The point here is to begin compiling a library of dreams that lives in your journal.

It's also recommended that once every week or two, you take time to go through your dream journal and read everything you have written. This process will jog your memory to recall these experiences, thus reinforcing the dreamlike state, which will again reward your mind to produce more of these types of experiences.

You'll know you're on the right track when you can record even just a single dream every day for a week without missing a day.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Notebook by the bed, every morning, before you touch your phone. Write anything, a fragment is enough. This one habit is the foundation everything else in this guide builds on.

Identifying Dream Signs

As you continue to document each dream interaction within your dream journal, you will likely notice something interesting: certain people, places, or items show up in a pattern across multiple dreams. Learning to identify these signs will make your job much easier while inside of a dream if you can recognize the pattern early on.

Dream signs are also highly personal, meaning they're unique to the individual and won't have much relevance from one person to another. In this way, dream signs will present differently to you, which is exactly why learning to take note of their appearance serves as a powerful tool to help you lucid dream.

Perhaps you often dream about a childhood memory or place you visited, a day that stuck out to you, or a person who means a great deal in your life. Whichever holds true to you, learning to identify the pattern in one dream to the next, you've found a dream sign which you should absolutely take note of and highlight in your dream journal entries. The power of a dream sign becomes even stronger when you can use it as a default reality check, specific to your recurring dream environment. If you can clock that experience as a pause-worthy moment to check your reality, then your dream sign will help you become lucid.

For example, if you have a recurring dream where you're back in grade school, you can use this to your advantage next time you find yourself in that environment by taking a moment to ask yourself if you're awake, or in a dream. If these patterns recur often, it will naturally become one of the most accurate indicators of you being in a dream or not.

Dream signs may even be more powerful than ordinary reality checks such as checking the time or looking at your hands, because the recurring nature of your environment is likely to tip you off. Take time every so often to sort through your dream journal to find these dream signs and make note of them, each one being a unique tripwire working to your benefit of pulling you into lucidity the next time you're dreaming.

The practice of becoming lucid directly from noticing a dream sign while in the middle of a dream is often referred to as dream-initiated lucid dreaming, DILD for short. Most of the induction methods we will cover in this guide are designed to increase the frequency of how often this happens naturally, with some taking a more direct route straight into a lucid dream while your mind is still awake and your body goes to sleep.

Writing Intentions

A large component of programming your mind to lucid dream is by training your subconscious mind to bend to your will. Unlike our conscious mind, the subconscious mind is highly malleable and open to suggestion, taking cues from our conscious mind each and every day. Therefore, the best way to shape our subconscious mind to allow us to lucid dream is by simply telling it to do so over and over.

By writing down your intentions to lucid dream, you are physically writing on paper what you want from your subconscious mind, and giving a direct instruction to be followed. This one sounds easy because it is: you simply think of a few variations of the message you want to encode, and then make a consistent effort to write them down every day. This could be as straightforward as “The next time I am dreaming, I will realize I'm dreaming” or “I lucid dream whenever I want” or “I am in control of my dreams whenever I want.”

Make writing down your intentions part of a daily practice alongside keeping a dream journal, and you're half of the way there towards achieving consistent lucid dreams. The remaining two parts come from changing the way you view your environments, and programming your body to work under your direction.

Reality Checks

Another core pillar to build our foundation for lucid dreaming is by making an effort to check our reality. Simply put, the dreamworld will feel extremely similar to our waking life, indistinguishable for most, that is, unless you have developed a framework for being able to consciously determine if you are in fact dreaming.

You may have noticed that certain objects in our waking life often do not show up in dreams as frequently as those in our waking lives: items such as phones, computers, and TV screens rarely appear in dreams. The reason being that many of these daily consumptions are practically ingrained within our subconscious mind: we check our phones religiously, we watch TV when we are bored, we surf the web mindlessly, these practices are baked into our subconscious mind, and therefore don't hold much significance when it is your subconscious mind that is creating a dream environment for you.

Nonetheless, there are several tested reality checks that you can and should begin practicing to build the habit of discerning if you are in a dream. In the same way we program our subconscious mind under the direction of our will, we will also program our mind to build the habit of identifying our environment and deciding if it's waking or dream state.

Light Switches

If you walk into a room and flip on a light switch inside of a dream, you'll find that the lights often do not turn on. Odd, but it usually seems to work. Similarly, if you check the time within a dream, on a wall clock, your watch, even digitally, the time won't look right. It's difficult to say exactly how it will present because dreams are highly personal to the individual, but rest assured, the time shown will look very different, in a way that's a dead giveaway you're in a dream state.

Checking Your Hands

Your hands are another prime example of this phenomenon, take a moment to stop reading and look at the palms of your hands. Everything looks normal and stable. In a dream, your hands will naturally start morphing and changing shape; you'll know immediately if you're dreaming or not.

Next up is breathing. Pinch your nose and try to breathe through it. Difficult, right? While you are in the middle of a dream, if you pinch your nose, you will have no issue breathing, despite pinching your dream nose. The rules of human anatomy or physics don't always apply in the dreamworld, so we'll use that to our advantage in deciding whether we're awake or dreaming.

Reading Text

If you look at any text within a dream: a title, a sentence, even a scribbled note, then look away, when you look back at it, it will likely have changed to something else entirely, or turned into unreadable gibberish. Text within dreams almost always changes the moment you look back at it.

Counting Your Fingers

Counting your fingers is another simple yet surprisingly powerful reality check to test out, because it seems so straightforward, yet the giveaway is always blatant. If you're dreaming and you hold your hand out to try counting your fingers, you'll notice the number of fingers on your hand is either more or less than normal, which should immediately alert you that something isn't right, and you're in a dream state. Give this reality test a try, you'll probably find your dream reaction is usually so obvious that you can become lucid immediately.

Touching Doorknobs

Finally, we have doorknobs. If you try to turn a doorknob or pull a door handle in a dream, it almost always won't work, and the knob or handle itself may change into something entirely different. In waking life, a doorknob that won't turn may not seem out of place, but in a dream, it's a telltale sign your environment is different, especially when it changes form or vanishes right in front of you.

Putting these examples together, the goal here is to start creating the habit of spending a few moments throughout your busy day to pause and ask yourself the big question: if this was a dream, would I even know it?

If the answer to that question is yes, then chances are you've had a lucid dream in your life, and this process should be relatively straightforward. If, however, that answer is no, then we'll need to start creating a waking practice of dream journaling, writing intentions, and performing reality checks to jumpstart this process from the ground up.

Reality Check Tips

One note here is there isn't necessarily an ideal number of reality checks to complete each day, instead, you should be focusing on quality over quantity. You'll know the reality checks are progressing when you perform them without any prompt such as an alarm, but rather as a sanity check whenever something strikes you as even slightly off.

One final mention on reality checks is to really lean into them as a foundation for lucid dreaming prep. Instead of making the action binary, dreaming or not, take a moment to survey your environment, turn and look around, take in your surroundings. Ask yourself where you were before this moment, or how did you get there?

If you want to elevate your reality checks even further, try to play the devil's advocate for why you may or may not be dreaming. Prove it to yourself by providing evidence and logic, instead of simply saying, my hands look fine, I'm not dreaming.

Expand on that idea. Maybe your hands look slightly different, why is that? Were you on a walk and have heightened circulation in your hands, maybe they're sweating a bit, but now you've stopped walking? Where was I before this? Does that sound right? Prove it to yourself, don't simply check the box and move on with your day.

Building Awareness

In dissecting reality checks and dream signs using our willed intention to build a sustainable practice, what all of this really boils down to is cultivating a deeper sense of awareness. What separates someone who effortlessly lucid dreams from someone who can't seem to get there is simply a state of mind around being aware of everything, including your surroundings, yourself, every single thing.

Easier said than done, but let's unpack what awareness really means. Start by asking basic questions: where am I? What's going on? How did I get here? Adopt a mentality of being cautiously optimistic, questioning things around you in a positive way.

The next step is including yourself in the mix as the observer of your surroundings. Your experience is subjective and creates your definition of reality, and acknowledging that is part of the process.

Finally, awareness is the entirety of this experience, both what's being observed, and the observation itself. You aren't separate from it, you simply are awareness, there's no disconnect between you and your surroundings at all.

Sleep Science

To fully grasp lucid dreaming, it helps to understand the mechanism of how we sleep. While this guide stays focused on lucid dream methodologies, here's a quick primer on sleep cycles: the time periods, frequencies, and cadences involved.

What you'll notice right off the bat is that our mind initially falls into levels of deeper sleep, then follows a pattern where our mind is near waking, before going back into deeper levels of sleep over and over again. What you'll also notice is that the longer we sleep, the longer our REM sleep cycles last, meaning the window to actually have a lucid dream also increases. This is one of the reasons why our dreams toward the morning seem to be more vivid or longer, the window is at its longest in the morning just before we wake, which is the perfect time to become lucid in a dream.

This is no mistake either, but instead, represents a ‘sleep cycle' where our mind goes through various levels of brainwave frequencies, which correspond to different levels of brain activity and rest. Instead of sleeping throughout the night at a particular level, our minds oscillate through roughly 90-minute cycles over the course of a night, allowing us to experience different levels of brainwave activity.

There are two types of sleep: REM sleep and non-REM (NREM) sleep. REM stands for rapid eye movement, and it's important to note here because REM sleep is the stage of our sleep cycle in which we typically dream. REM sleep is characterized by brief windows of time where your brain is actually as active as when you're awake, and even more so when you're lucid. You can also have dreams in NREM stages, however this typically occurs for those who tend to sleepwalk or verbalize their dreams.

Our normal, waking brain frequency consists of beta waves, which indicate high mental activity, presented with fast, low-amplitude brainwaves vibrating throughout the day between 13–30 Hz. Just before sleep, while we're relaxed with our eyes closed but still technically awake, our brain shifts into alpha waves: a calm, rhythmic pattern between 8–13 Hz, that drowsy, drifting-off feeling right before you actually fall asleep.

Once we cross into actual sleep, we enter stage one, or N1, the lightest stage of sleep, marked by theta waves between 4–7 Hz. This is a brief transitional stage, often lasting just a few minutes, where you can be woken easily and might not even realize you'd fallen asleep.

From there we move into stage two, or N2, still marked by theta brainwaves, but now experiencing brief bursts of brain activity called sleep spindles and K-complexes, your brain's way of processing the outside world and staying lightly tuned to your environment even while asleep.

Compare this to stage three, or N3, which consists of delta brainwaves, operating between 0.5–4 Hz, the deepest level of sleep possible. During delta sleep, we become difficult to wake as our blood pressure drops, our body begins repairing tissue, and our metabolism slows.

The reason to highlight the fluctuating nature of our sleep cycle is to illustrate that certain times are better than others to successfully induce a lucid dream, not all sleep is equal. The goal is to focus our efforts on maximizing the opportunity zone to lucid dream while we are in REM sleep, since this has the highest chance of allowing our consciousness to awaken in the middle of a dream.

REM Rebounds

One topic to mention is the REM Rebound reaction, which basically means when you've deprived your sleep, your brain will increase the length of REM sleep to compensate for any missed or lost sleep. You can use REM rebounds to your advantage if your goal is to have longer windows for lucid dreaming, but it comes as the cost of depriving yourself of sleep.

Since dream time is relative to the time scale of our waking life, you could in theory use a REM rebound period to entertain a longer lucid dream, but know that you will be putting your brain into overtime to achieve this, so if you feel that your dreams are too short and you'd like to extend them further, it's recommended you only try REM rebounding when you have more time to catch up on sleep, such as a weekend or holiday.

If you do want to attempt REM rebounding, there's two ways to get there, but really only one option makes the most sense to optimize for sleep. The first option is to simply stay awake for a period of around 24 hours without any sleep, but this is highly taxing on your body and brain, and should likely be avoided.

The second option, which is likely the better outcome, is to set an alarm a few hours after falling asleep, so you get some rest, but not enough to enter deeper levels of prolonged sleep. We'll explore this method in more detail shortly, but the premise is to wake up prematurely, stay awake for a short while, then go back to bed, extending your window of REM sleep beyond what you'd normally get.

Hand Placement

A quick note on hand placement, stemming from my own personal experience and a relatively high success rate for lucid dream induction. If you're right handed, place your right hand over your heart and your left hand over your right hip, lying down on your back before sleep.

If you're left-handed, reverse the order and place your left hand on your right chest and your right hand on your left hip. I have found that this hand placement will give your body a more natural ease into restful sleep, while your mind can focus on the vertical rhythm of your hand going up and down created by your diaphragm expanding and contracting as you breathe.

Give this hand placement a shot and see if it feels comfortable for you. I personally have noticed a huge uptick in lucid dreaming outcomes when I position my hands this way, and it's worth trying especially when you're first starting out.

Sleep Habits

It's worth mentioning that when you're developing the skillset to lucid dream at will, you'll need to become more consistent in your practice, which includes sufficient time to actually sleep. Sleep is one of the most important parts of our lives, so plan your day around getting enough of it.

Each individual has a different need for adequate rest, and while the goal of this guide isn't to tell you how much sleep you should be getting, it's worth aiming for consistency from one night to the next. Give yourself a predefined window you can stick to for an extended period of time.

We're all human, so don't expect perfection every day, but aim to consistently go to bed and wake up around the same time. Lucid dreaming aside, your body will thank you for the shift, since we're habit-forming creatures that thrive on routine.

Prioritize your sleeping hours, establish a healthy pre-sleep and morning routine, and avoid screens before bed and right after waking. Treat your sleep as the recharge mechanism for your longevity, the more you invest in it, the more you'll get out of life.

A First Attempt: What This Actually Looks Like

When you're first starting out, it certainly helps to see how these pieces fit together in practice, so let's walk through what a first attempt might realistically look like using the MILD method.

You've been keeping your dream journal for about three weeks, and you've started noticing a repeating dream sign: you find yourself back in your childhood home, a place you haven't lived in for over a decade.

You've also been doing your reality checks throughout the day, mostly checking your hands and glancing at clocks twice to see if the time actually changes. Tonight, before bed, you decide to pair the MILD method with that dream sign specifically.

As you lie down in bed, you close your eyes and picture yourself walking through the front door of your childhood home. You visualize looking down at your hands, noticing they look slightly wrong, the color and texture isn't quite right, and in that moment you say to yourself: "The next time I'm in this house, I'll realize I'm dreaming." You run through that same scene two or three more times, each time feeling a little more confident that moment will actually happen and you'll be ready for when it does.

You fall asleep as you normally would. A few hours later, you do find yourself back in that house, walking through the same doorway you've walked through several times in other dreams. Except this time, something makes you pause. You glance down at your hands, almost out of habit, and there it is, your hands are morphing in front of your eyes. The question in your mind arises, could I be dreaming?

You want to be sure, though, so you check the clock on the wall, and it looks strange, the minute and hour hands don't look like you remember. You turn away and look back again, and the clock looks completely different. This confirms your suspicion: you're dreaming, and you feel peaceful, you've finally arrived.

That's it. There's no dramatic effect, no jolt of energy, only a quiet moment of realization that closes the loop about if any of this was real in the first place. From there, you're free to do whatever you'd like: keep exploring the house, try flying out a window, or simply stand still and take in the fact that you actually did it. This is what all of the practice is building towards, a feeling of knowing that you won't forget any time soon.

Wake Back To Bed (WBTB) Method

This brings us to one of the main induction techniques for lucid dreaming: the wake back to bed (WBTB) method. The process here is straightforward: wake up earlier than you normally would via an alarm system, get out of bed, do something simple yet not too strenuous, then go back to bed. It's important to note that what you do after you wake up has a direct effect on your ability to go back to sleep, so do your best to stay off screens or begin your daily routine, because we want to trick our mind into thinking we are completing a one-off task, not starting our morning.

Examples of this would be to use the restroom, do your dishes from the night before, have a glass of water, take a short walk outside, anything that puts your mind into the ‘well, this sure is a side quest' mentality. Aim to take about ten minutes before you return to bed.

Once you're back in bed, commit to this process and use your will to fall back asleep. If you were able to recall a dream you had overnight, re-read that section of your dream journal and remember what that dream felt like: everything from the sensations you felt, to the imagery, setting, colors, relive that moment like you are experiencing it again right now.

As you lie in bed, put your mind back into the dream. Even if your body isn't buying this experience as real, use your imagination to visualize the dream as it is. Relax into this process, don't stress out over having to make all the pieces line up perfectly. By doing this, your chances of re-entering a dream state are tenfold immediately, your body is primed to sleep, and now your mind is ready to dream.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Wake early, do something mundane for ~10 minutes, then go back to bed and re-live your last remembered dream as you fall back asleep.

A Note on Alarms

If you decide to use one of the above methods, which requires waking up at a certain time, and your first thought is to set an alarm on your phone, take a pause here. Do yourself a favor and purchase a simple alarm clock that can sit next to your bedside, preferably one that is not very bright or distracting. For lack of a better term, find a boring alarm clock and use this instead of a phone.

The reason to use an alarm clock as opposed to a phone is that often, when using a smartphone, our natural tendency is to multitask and dive down a rabbit hole of a million different side quests. Ideally, move your phone out of your bedroom and place it somewhere else. Not only does scrolling online before bed hurt your ability to sleep, but it also becomes a crutch when a notebook isn't nearby.

Mnemonic-Induced Lucid Dreaming (MILD) Method

Let's dig into another effective method to achieve lucidity in dreams: mnemonic-induced lucid dreaming (MILD). The theme here is to combine setting an intention with a repeated mantra while falling asleep, to help your mind become lucid the next time you're in a dream.

As mentioned previously, reality checks are a daily task that should be baked into your practice by this point. For this example, let's take one reality check and pair it with an intention to restructure how it feels to accomplish the task. Let's use flipping a light switch when you walk into a room, immediately followed by the mantra “The next time I'm dreaming, I will realize I'm dreaming.”

When you lie down to sleep, imagine a scenario where you're walking into a room, you see a light switch, and you naturally flip the switch. You say to yourself, “The next time I'm dreaming, I will realize I'm dreaming.” Something clicks in your mind as you say this, you're not performing a reality check, you're simply observing that you're in a dream, and at this moment, you finally realize it.

Run that same scenario in your mind, visualizing the action paired with a verbal intention. The logic here is to continually run this scenario as you fall asleep, preparing your subconscious mind to arrive at the same conclusion your conscious mind reaches during visualization.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Pick one reality check, pair it with a mantra (“The next time I'm dreaming, I will realize I'm dreaming”), and visualize the two together as you fall asleep.

Recall-Induced Lucid Dreaming (RILD) Method

The next method to try out is the recall-induced lucid dreaming method, which is actually more simple than it sounds. To attempt this method, you'll want to start by going through your dream journal and picking an entry that feels particularly clear or memorable, one that has good context about how you felt, both leading up to and during the experience.

Read through that entry, and read it back again before you go to sleep. When reading through the dream journal, try to imagine the sensations of being in the actual dream. As you lay down and get ready to sleep, you're going to try recalling that dream again, every moment of it: the start, the middle, the end.

This method is very powerful when done consistently, you're priming your mind to revisit these moments of an immersive and intense dream state, and connecting the dots to relive those moments in your current awareness. As you drift off to sleep, if you're able to retain the recollection of the dream and play it back in your mind just as you would watch a movie, the chances of you directly dropping into a lucid dream skyrocket.

If you find it too difficult to remember all the parts of a dream, try to focus on a specific section, and relive each moment of that dream. Even if the dream you're remembering was not lucid in nature, you're getting infinitely closer to achieving a lucid dream by tuning in to the feelings, experiences, and mindset that comes with paying attention to your dreams at length.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Pick a vivid dream journal entry, re-read it before bed, then mentally replay it in full detail as you fall asleep: start to middle to end.

Senses-Induced Lucid Dreaming (SSILD) Method

The final lucid dreaming induction we'll cover before WILD is the senses-induced method. If the idea of staying awake through sleep paralysis sounds a bit intense, this induction technique will likely be a better option, since it doesn't require that at all.

In some ways, the SSILD method is similar to the WBTB method, because you'll begin by waking up in the morning a bit earlier than you normally would, then staying awake for about ten minutes, getting up and moving around, before returning to bed.

Once you're back in bed and settled in, close your eyes and focus your full attention on your ability to see. Despite your eyelids being closed, you will likely notice small details: light patterns, static, or just pure darkness. Sit with these sensations and simply observe them. Breathe rhythmically in whatever way feels comfortable, but keep your attention on your sight.

After a few minutes, completely shift your focus to your sense of hearing. Observe what you hear, even something as slight as air conditioning in your room, distant traffic sounds, or the sounds your body makes as you breathe. Hold your attention here for a few minutes.

Finally, shift your attention to your sense of touch while you lay in bed: the firmness of your pillow, the softness of the sheets, the movement of your chest as it rises and falls with each breath.

Combining these three objectives creates one cycle of around five or so minutes; repeat this cycle about five times as you drift off to sleep. You'll likely notice you become more and more relaxed with each cycle, which is the goal, for your body to drift off to sleep while your mind retains its awareness of your senses.

This method works surprisingly well because, unlike the more demanding WILD method covered next, you're not asking your body to fight off sleep, you're actually welcoming it. Think of SSILD as a halfway point between the passiveness of MILD and the intensity of WILD.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Wake briefly, then cycle through sight → sound → touch (about 5 minutes each) as you drift back to sleep. Repeat the cycle roughly five times.

Before You Begin: Sleep Paralysis

It's worth noting that induction methods requiring you to stay mentally awake as your body drifts off can trigger sleep paralysis, a normal step in the sleep cycle, not a scary one.

While we sleep and move around in our dream environment, our actual body lays still in bed, paralyzed to keep us from physically acting out our dreams, which could otherwise mean dangerous sleepwalking.

The body begins with random jerking sensations as our mind drifts off, then a series of sensations follow: itching, chest weight, a faster heart rate, or warmth throughout the body.

Moving during this process alerts the brain you're not ready to sleep, resetting things. Staying calm and breathing normally instead lets you progress through the final stage, often described as strange or otherworldly: odd sounds, a feeling of being watched, or pressure that varies by person.

YOU ARE SAFE

Whatever it feels like, understand that you are completely safe, and sleep paralysis is temporary and will pass. Instead of giving in to fear or arousal, stay focused on your induction method, breathe normally, and let the sensations pass. If you feel extremely bothered, you can move a finger, bat an eyelid, or change your breathing to stop sleep paralysis in its tracks and wake yourself up immediately.

Wake-Induced Lucid Dreaming (WILD) Method

The final method we'll cover here is wake-induced lucid dreaming (WILD). It takes a bit more finesse, but gives you greater control over keeping your mind awake while commanding your body to sleep, and is best practiced right after a good night's rest, first thing in the morning.

Same as the WBTB method, take a few moments to re-read your recent journal entries and remember what each dream felt like. Lie down, close your eyes, and let the morning mind chatter pass, simply observing these thoughts, separate from them, keeping your focus on breathing gently.

One helpful tool if you're struggling to quiet your mind is an anchored visualization: imagine your name spelled out in three-dimensional characters, viewed from a distance, gently moving, like watching letters of alphabet soup float in a bowl. If you're focusing on your eyes staying shut, you may experience random visual patterns called hypnagogia, of no particular importance, simply observe them until they pass. Once you see nothing, think nothing, and feel your body starting to drift, you're right where you want to be.

For the final step, focus on a place or setting, it doesn't even need to be real. Look around, take a step forward, wave your arms in front of you, and tell yourself that you're dreaming. Finally, will yourself to front flip into this new environment, it sounds odd, but it works.

If you've gotten this far, chances are you're not only lucid dreaming, but in a dream you consciously constructed. Don't worry if you've struggled to get here, these things take time, and building the right framework matters more than speed.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Wake, re-read a journal entry, quiet your mind with an anchored visualization, then build and step into a specific setting once your body starts to drift.

Choosing Your Starting Method

Now that we've covered all five lucid dreaming induction methods, it's fair to wonder where to actually begin. Here's a simple way to think about it.

If you're starting from zero, you'd likely be best beginning with the MILD method: it's easy to initiate and gives you a straightforward, repeatable script to follow without needing any special sleep setup. Once you've built a consistent sleep schedule, pair it with the WBTB method, using a brief wake-up to get you right to that cusp between awake and asleep.

If you're more intermediate, already keeping a dream journal with some dream recall established, the RILD method will likely serve you best, and should probably become your default.

If you're approaching lucid dreaming as more passive and low intensity, with no rush to see progress, SSILD is a great option, similar to meditation, where the goal is simply to exist in the moment.

If you want added intensity and a more immersive experience, WILD offers the most direct path to lucidity, but takes the most discipline. If you haven't had success with other methods, hold off on this one until you've made more progress.

There really is no wrong choice here, and you're by no means locked into one option, more advanced dreamers often rotate between methods depending on their energy or mood. Give yourself room to try, fail, learn, and regroup without judging each method individually.

LUCID DREAMING

The Lucid Dreaming Protocol

In learning to lucid dream, stay consistent: checking reality, recording dreams, writing intentions, attempting inductions, and keeping a steady sleep schedule. These five habits train your subconscious mind toward consistent lucid dreams.

If your goal is to have your first lucid dream without any external stimulus, you'll need to develop a daily practice and stick to it for no less than two months, since life gets busy and it's easy to fall a few days behind.

While 60 days is a solid benchmark, aim for around 90 days of consistent practice, since new habits take real time to form and can't be rushed. We're not just forming a habit, we're rewiring your subconscious mind to induce lucid dreams on command.

This process takes time, it cannot be rushed, and there's no shortcut to it. Give it your all: make an effort each day, and make every action count, rather than just checking a box in your dream journal.

As human beings aren't perfect, it's fair to miss a day occasionally, just make sure it stays an isolated event: missing one day is normal, missing two in a row creates a new habit, so reset the clock and start fresh.

One last nugget: going back to sleep right when you wake up is one of the best times to lucid dream, since you're most alert and aware. We wake after a night of dreams, so that's the optimal window, rather than at night.

Your First Lucid Dream

You've finally managed to have your first lucid dream. Congratulations, that's a big step. What do you do now?

After becoming lucid in a dream, the next most important thing is staying lucid in that dream, not waking up or falling back into the dream without your full awareness at hand.

Oftentimes we get overly excited once we become lucid, and that excitement becomes the reason we're jolted awake. Be calm: your goal should be to arrive at a lucid dream confidently and gently.

Three Tricks to Stay Lucid

  1. Pause. Stop whatever you're doing and simply acknowledge that you're in a dream, calmly and gently.
  2. Rub your hands together while spinning in a circle. This strengthens your connection to your surroundings, making them feel tangible.
  3. Set a vocal intention: something like “Clarity times 100 now!” This directly imprints on your subconscious mind, sharpening every detail of the dream.

If done in sequence, the erratic timescale of a dream will slow down and feel much more lifelike, because now you're lucid and your subconscious mind is working overtime to match your dreamworld to reality. From here on, the real fun starts.

Lucid Yet Not in Control

One thing to point out here is that it is quite possible to become lucid in a dream, yet not be in control of the actual dream. You can very well know that you're dreaming, yet unable to actually steer the direction of the dream toward what you want, and that can be quite jarring in the moment.

If this does happen to you, know that the feeling of being aware but not in control is actually your decision, and while it does take a bit of confidence to take hold and bend a lucid dream to your will, just know that you've already won, the hard part is over.

Try vocalizing what you'd like to do in the dream, make your intentions prominently known, and you'll likely find that your subconscious mind is happy to bend to whatever your conscious mind commands.

Lucid Dream Actions

There's certainly no shortage of things to do in a lucid dream, but let's highlight a few particularly noteworthy items.

Setting a Lucid Dream Intention

Before you decide to induce a lucid dream, it's worth deciding if there's something specific you want to experience or accomplish the next time you find yourself lucid in your dream. Typically people will spend a ton of time and energy on getting to the point where you can lucid dream, that they forget to stop and direct the experience as they most desire. What ends up happening there is you'll likely default to a handful of similar actions, perhaps you fly around for a bit, teleport somewhere, and then the dream comes to an end. By setting an intention in advance, in the same way you write your intentions in a journal before bed, you can give your lucid dreams a sense of purpose beyond the novelty of arriving in a dream randomly.

We discussed lucid dreaming purposes earlier in this guide, but it's worth revisiting now to reinforce the idea of intentions both awake and while you're lucid in a dream. If you're practicing lucid dreaming to rehearse a skill, or navigate a scenario, imagine yourself actually stepping into that reality the moment you become aware in a dream. If you're working through something subconscious, whether it's a recurring environment or a question you can't seem to find the answer to in your waking life, make your intention to ask the question you want answered or the issue you want solved. You don't need to overthink this process either, it's really for your own organization and structure to stay focused once you become lucid. Set a clear intention beforehand so you're not caught flat-footed wondering what to do with the lucidity you worked so hard for.

False Awakenings

If you're consistently practicing these steps to achieve your first lucid dream, you may find yourself in a circumstance called a false awakening: essentially where you're dreaming but think you're awake, going about your day as normal without considering you could be in a dream. If this happens, it means you're on the right path, but we need to adjust your protocol a bit.

First, false awakenings mean your reality checks are not taking hold as much as we'd like, and they need to be reinforced in your waking life so they can carry over to your dream state. It's recommended that you slow down your reality checks to allow them to sit deeper in your mind, instead of rushing past them to go on with your day.

Also, take some time to wander around a bit shortly after doing a reality check, as this may jog a dream sign or reinforce your suspicion that your surroundings are a dream. For example, if you're in a house and touch a doorknob, walk into other rooms and do a 360 scan of your surroundings, trying to pinpoint any small differences.

When a false awakening occurs, it typically means that your dream will feel very similar to your waking life, and therefore you need to give extra attention to deciding if you're dreaming or not. False awakenings will also become quite common as you start having more lucid dreams, in fact you'll become much more aware of recurring environments, so better to develop the skill to point them out sooner rather than later.

Navigating a Lucid Dream

First off, flying. It's incredibly fun and immersive to fly through any environment, but it takes a bit of finesse to get started. The key is to remain relaxed, in the same way you might float in a swimming pool. The more you let go and focus on what's above you, the more your dream body naturally wants to take flight.

If you're having difficulty letting go and feel stuck on the ground, start out by making large jumps to nearby destinations: a nearby roof, a mountaintop, anything that looks like you can physically stand there, jump, and land smoothly. Once you're higher up, try gliding down with your arms extended.

The next task you might want to try is teleportation. Start by walking up to a closed door and setting an intention of where you want to go, this destination could be anywhere. Tell yourself out loud where this door will open up to, then simply turn the handle and walk through. You'll be amazed at what your mind can create in a moment's notice.

Seeking Guidance from a Dream Figure

One of the most meaningful experiences you can try when you're lucid in a dream is to seek out a figure or a person in a dream and have a conversation with them directly. This could be anyone or anything in a dream, and since it's all your own subconscious projected at you, these beings often carry insights into your daily life that your conscious mind doesn't have access to while you're awake.

If you'd like to try this, once lucid in a dream, simply make a verbal intention and ask to speak with someone who can offer advice or guidance, or feel free to approach anyone in the dream and ask them directly. You'll likely be surprised by the level of clarity or accuracy in their responses, or how a being reacts to being asked something personal or sincere. This practice ties back directly with the self-exploration and purpose section mentioned earlier in this guide, and it's truly one of the most rewarding actions you can take once you've built a practice of consistently becoming and staying lucid in a dream.

Reshaping Your Environment

While flying and teleporting throughout the dreamworld are both fun ways to move in a lucid dream, it's also worth remembering that you are the conscious creator of these experiences, and you can literally change your environment at will. Try walking through a solid wall while you state your intention to pass through it, or focus specifically on an object and will it to change form, size, or material right in front of your eyes. There are no limits to what you can or cannot control, as long as you retain the confidence in yourself to believe anything is possible.

The more you can practice altering small details such as color, texture, or feelings, then you'll develop the ability to change entire environments with ease. This is the point where lucid dreaming becomes less of a specific technique you perform, and more of a space that you fully control with so much as a thought.

You can also explore beyond moving or reshaping what already exists, and try out bringing something into existence out of nothing at all. To conjure something, reach your hand out and set an intention to hold a specific object, and it will simply appear in your hand. The same logic goes for all of your surroundings, you can will an empty space to be built from the ground up and filled with everything you want instantly.

Once you start operating as a powerful creator in your dreams, you're at the center of these experiences, and you're no longer working with what your subconscious mind randomly created for you, you're directing it all on your own. The abilities you possess have no end once you realize that they've been there for you the entire time, and your dream is now your own personal domain to explore anything and everything you've ever wanted. Have fun with these experiences, see how far you can push the bounds of your new reality.

Strengthening a Lucid Dream

One of the best things you can do to strengthen your dream state is actually to tell others you're in a lucid dream. People around you, friends, family members, they're all creations of your subconscious mind, and telling them you're dreaming means they'll likely help you retain lucidity, or even remind you that you're still lucid.

One final suggestion: while in a lucid dream, look at yourself in a mirror. Experiences vary widely, but generally, this produces an introspective look into your subconscious view of your own appearance.

As with all suggestions, go at your own pace, and don't be discouraged if you can't fit everything into a single lucid dream. Once you wake up, take time to document and reflect on your experiences: treat each one as a lesson, take thoughtful notes, and revisit them down the road.

If a Dream Turns Unsettling

Occasionally, a lucid dream can take a turn toward something unsettling or fearful. Perhaps you encounter a threatening entity, an unknown environment, or a negative gut feeling you didn't expect. If this happens, remember that you are always safe, and nothing that happens in your dream can physically hurt you.

Because you're lucid, you have far more control over the situation than you may realize in the moment.

Options When a Dream Turns Unsettling

  1. Remind yourself out loud: “I am in control, this can’t hurt me.”
  2. Change your surroundings, walk through a door and set an intention for a calmer, safer place on the other side.
  3. Turn and face whatever feels threatening. Confronting it head-on, rather than running, often causes it to lose its menacing nature.
  4. Choose to wake yourself up. Moving your body, opening your eyes, or simply willing yourself awake will end the dream immediately.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: I keep having these recurring dreams. I want to control them, but I can’t seem to become lucid. How can I break this pattern?

A: Your best bet is to make the documentation process extremely detailed, record everything you can recall, including how you're feeling. While our mind will do its best to re-create recurring dreams, these dreams are actually slightly different variations of each other. It's your job to observe those differences and piece together the fact that you are dreaming. In many ways, recurring dreams are particularly helpful for achieving lucid dreams because you get the same baseline experience over and over, giving you a great chance to perform reality checks.

Q: I can’t seem to gain the awareness in a dream to perform a reality check. What am I doing wrong?

A: This is a common issue beginners run into, and it stems from repetition, not natural ability. Reality checks will only work within your dreams if you've built a near-automatic system so ingrained that your subconscious has adapted. If you're only completing a few reality checks daily, your subconscious hasn't yet logged enough repetitions to build the habit. If checks become a ‘check the box’ action, slow down and truly ask yourself if your environment is real, evaluate it as if you're a skeptic who needs convincing. This usually takes a few weeks to click in, so give yourself grace.

Q: How long will it actually take before I have my first lucid dream?

A: This changes from person to person, and comparing your results to someone else's is a negative thought loop not worth entertaining. Some people have their first lucid dream within days or weeks of practice, others take months. What matters more than the end result is finding joy in the progress leading up to it, recording your dreams, practicing reality checks, writing intentions, and attempting the induction methods are all improving your dream recall and your relationship with your higher self along the way, regardless of when the first lucid dream actually lands.

Q: Do I need any supplements, devices, or big lifestyle changes to lucid dream?

A: No, and that's intentional. This guide was written as a simple methodology to teach you how to lucid dream through consistency and willpower without any external crutches. While there are claims of supplements, devices, and apps that may aid the process, I'd remain highly skeptical and actively warn against relying on anything to support this process. Millions of people have lucid dreams every night without any outside intervention. My goal is for you to learn this skill naturally and on your own, so you can carry it with you for the rest of your life without needing to buy or take anything.

Q: I'm having trouble staying engaged to continue practicing each day, what can I do to stay motivated?

A: Imagine the first time you heard about lucid dreaming, and how amazing that sensation felt. The idea that you could consciously control your dreams, fly like a superhero, work through problems by talking with your subconscious mind, facing fears from recurring dreams or nightmares. Use these feelings as motivation to keep pushing you forward, and know that once you start having more consistent lucid dreams, then all of these practices will be much more passive to keep your mental knives sharp as opposed to learning a new skill. You're likely closer than you think to having your first lucid dream and actualizing your goals, so stay in it.

Glossary

Alpha Waves

The calm, rhythmic brainwaves, between 8 and 13 Hz, present while you're relaxed with your eyes closed but still awake: that drowsy feeling right before you fall asleep.

Beta Waves

The fast, low-amplitude brainwaves associated with normal, alert waking consciousness, present right up until you begin drifting toward sleep.

Delta Waves

The slow brainwaves, between 0.5 and 4 Hz, that mark N3, the deepest stage of non-REM sleep.

DILD (Dream-Initiated Lucid Dreaming)

Becoming lucid spontaneously from noticing a dream sign while already inside a dream, rather than from actively inducing it. Most methods in this guide aim to increase how often this happens naturally.

Dream Journal

A notebook kept by your bedside to record every dream you can recall upon waking, the single most foundational habit for consistent lucid dreaming.

Dream Recall

Your ability to remember a dream after waking up, strengthened directly through consistent use of a dream journal.

Dream Sign

A person, place, object, or pattern that recurs across your dreams. Recognizing your personal dream signs is one of the most reliable ways to become lucid.

False Awakening

A dream in which you believe you've woken up and resumed your normal waking life, only to realize later you're still dreaming, more common as your practice progresses.

Hypnagogia

The random visual patterns, shapes, or flashes you may notice as your mind drifts toward sleep with your eyes closed: harmless, and worth simply noticing.

Lucid Dream

A dream in which you become consciously aware that you are dreaming, giving you the ability to direct or influence what happens next.

MILD (Mnemonic-Induced Lucid Dreaming)

An induction method that pairs a reality check with a repeated mantra as you fall asleep, training your subconscious to recognize the dream state.

N1, N2, N3

The three progressively deeper stages of non-REM sleep your mind cycles through each night, each marked by a different brainwave pattern.

Nightmare

A distressing dream, often one that jolts you awake. Becoming lucid gives you the tools to change course mid-nightmare rather than simply enduring it.

Reality Check

A quick habitual test, checking a clock, your hands, or your breath, used to determine whether you're awake or dreaming.

REM Rebound

A phenomenon where sleep deprivation causes your brain to lengthen REM sleep afterward to compensate, which can extend your window for lucid dreaming at the cost of sleep.

REM Sleep

Rapid Eye Movement sleep, the stage of the sleep cycle in which most dreaming occurs, and the best window for lucid dream induction.

RILD (Recall-Induced Lucid Dreaming)

An induction method built around vividly recalling a past dream journal entry as you fall asleep, priming your mind to re-enter a similar dream state.

Sleep Cycle

One full pass through the stages of sleep, from light sleep through deep sleep and into REM, repeating roughly every 90 minutes through the night.

Sleep Paralysis

A natural, temporary state where your body stays physically still while your mind remains partially or fully awake, common during WILD attempts and completely safe.

SSILD (Senses-Induced Lucid Dreaming)

A gentle induction method that cycles attention through sight, sound, and touch while drifting back to sleep.

Subconscious Mind

The layer of your mind beneath your everyday waking awareness, responsible for constructing your dreams and highly receptive to what your conscious mind feeds it.

Theta Waves

The brainwave pattern, between 4 and 7 Hz, present during N1 and N2 sleep, as well as during the drowsy, hypnagogic state just before sleep.

Visualization

Vividly imagining a scene or outcome in your mind, a core skill used throughout this guide, from recalling dream signs to rehearsing a lucid dream before sleep.

WBTB (Wake Back To Bed)

An induction method involving briefly waking during the night, staying up a short while, then returning to bed to re-enter sleep with a sharper mind.

WILD (Wake-Induced Lucid Dreaming)

An advanced induction method in which you remain consciously aware as your body falls asleep, transitioning directly into a lucid dream.
